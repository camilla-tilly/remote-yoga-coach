## Draft 1: the ninety seconds nobody moves

I taught a breathing class at Bruce Studios last week, and for the first
ninety seconds of it, nobody moves.

That's on purpose. In four, hold for four, out for six. No pose, no
demonstration, just counting people down until their shoulders drop about an
inch without anyone telling them to.

I watch for that drop more than anything else I teach. It's the tell that
someone has actually left their inbox for a minute, not just closed the tab.

What surprises people who haven't tried it is how little happens. No
stretching, no floor work, nobody needs to change clothes. You could do the
whole thing in a desk chair with your shoes still on.

Half the reason people skip these sessions, I think, is that they've never
actually seen one. It looks like nothing from the outside. That's kind of the
point.

## Draft 2 (alternative, same type): chair yoga, five minutes in

Five minutes into a chair yoga class, most of the room has stopped checking
whether they're doing it right.

That's the shift I look for. The first few minutes are self-conscious,
people glancing sideways, copying the person next to them instead of feeling
what their own body is doing. Then it just quietens down.

We're not doing anything dramatic. A seated twist, a neck release, arms
overhead and back down. The kind of movement that undoes an hour of hunching
over a laptop, nothing that needs a change of clothes or a mat.

Cameras are always optional. Some people join fully off, and the room still
works exactly the same, because nobody in a chair yoga class is performing
for anyone else.

It's a strange thing to teach, movement that's meant to be almost invisible.
But it's the invisible ones people ask to repeat.
